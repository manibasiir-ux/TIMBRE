Most sonic identities fail eighteen months after delivery, and almost none of
them fail because the music was wrong.

They fail because a regional marketing lead needed a fifteen-second cut for a
campaign and nobody had made one, so somebody made one badly. They fail because
a new agency was briefed on the visual identity and never told there was an
audio one. They fail because the person who cared left.

> The work is not finished when the sound is finished. It is finished when
> someone who has never met you can use it correctly without asking.

## What we hand over

The audio is the easy half. The other half is a set of documents nobody enjoys
writing:

- A naming convention, applied to every file, with the version and the loudness
  target in the filename itself
- A format matrix: which encoding goes to which platform, and what happens to
  the mix at each one
- A decision list — not "use the brand tone here" but "the confirmation tone
  plays on success only, never on submit, and never twice in one flow"
- A page on what to do when something is missing, naming a person

That last one matters more than it sounds. Most misuse is not defiance. It is
somebody at 6pm with a deadline and no way to ask.

## The test

We use one question to check whether a handover is real: **could a marketing
manager in another country, who has never spoken to us, brief an external editor
correctly using only the documents?**

If the answer needs a phone call, it is not a system yet. It is a folder.

## Why this is in the price

Clients occasionally ask to remove the guidelines from a proposal to bring the
cost down. We do not do it, and the reason is not principle.

An identity without governance produces a second project in two years — a
re-derivation of work that already exists, at full cost, because nobody could
tell what was intended. The guidelines are cheaper than the rebuild, and we would
rather sell the cheaper thing.

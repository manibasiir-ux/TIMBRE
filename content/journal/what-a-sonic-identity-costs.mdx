We argued about this for two hours before launch, which is longer than we argued
about anything else on the site.

The case against publishing prices is real. Bands make a studio look like a
commodity. They invite comparison on a number rather than on the work. They
anchor a client low before you have had a chance to explain what the work
involves.

The case for is simpler: silence makes it a phone call, and phone calls take
three weeks to schedule.

## What actually happens without a number

A marketing director is putting a budget together. They need a figure for
"sonic identity" by Friday. They find four studios, none of whom publish
anything, and they have time to have one conversation.

They either pick the studio somebody has heard of, or they put in a number they
have guessed. If they guess low, the project arrives underfunded and everyone is
disappointed. If they guess high, it does not get approved.

Neither of those outcomes is improved by our discretion.

## Why bands and not prices

A fixed price would be a lie. The same brief costs differently depending on how
many territories it has to work in, whether there is an existing catalogue to
audit, and whether the client has a functioning approvals process.

A band says: this is the order of magnitude, and here is what moves it. That is
enough to build a budget around, which is all anyone needed.

## The part that surprised us

Publishing bands did not reduce the number of enquiries. It changed who was
sending them. Fewer people asking what it costs, more people describing what
they need — because the ones who could not afford it had already left, and the
ones who could had skipped a step.

We would have been happy with fewer, better enquiries. We got the same number,
better.

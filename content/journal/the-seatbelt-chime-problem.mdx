The seatbelt chime took eleven versions. The boarding bed, four minutes long and
heard by every passenger on every flight, took five.

That ratio is not unusual and it is worth explaining, because it is the clearest
example of something that governs most of this work: the difficulty of a sound
has almost nothing to do with its length.

## An instruction is not a mood

A boarding bed has one job: make a cabin feel like somewhere you have chosen to
be. It can be wrong in a dozen ways and still work, because nobody is required
to act on it.

A seatbelt chime is an instruction. It has to cut through conversation, a cabin
noise floor around 65 dB, and whatever is playing through somebody's headphones.
It has to be recognised in under a second by a passenger who is not listening for
it. And it has to do that without sounding like an alarm, because a cabin full of
people who think something is wrong is worse than a cabin full of people who did
not hear anything at all.

Those requirements pull against each other. Cutting through noise wants high
frequency and sharp attack. Not sounding like an alarm wants the opposite.

## What eleven versions actually were

Versions one to four were us treating it as a design problem. They were, in
order: too soft, too urgent, too similar to a competitor's, and rather good but
inaudible at the back of a 737.

Versions five to nine were us treating it as an acoustics problem. We tested
against recorded cabin noise at three aircraft types and two volumes. Two
candidates survived.

Versions ten and eleven were us treating it as a human problem. We played both
to people who had not been told what they were listening to and asked what they
thought they should do. One of them made people look up. The other made them
reach for something.

That is the whole test, and it is not a test you can run on an idea.

## The general case

Every project has one asset like this. On a payments product it was a failure
tone — the one sound that has to be understood while somebody is already
anxious. On a hotel system it was the sound a door makes when a key does not
work, which people hear at midnight with luggage.

Look for the asset where being misunderstood has a cost. It will be short, it
will be the least interesting thing in the deck, and it will take three times
longer than anything else.

Budget for it accordingly.
